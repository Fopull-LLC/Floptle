# Floptle v0.44.0 — "Draw Over It"

Your own full-screen effects. Write a shader that gets the finished frame — its
colours, how far away everything is, and which way each surface faces — and hand
back a new picture. Drop as many as you like on the Post Processing node, in the
order you want them.

One of them ships built in: a comic-book ink outline around everything in the
scene, ready to switch on, and written to be read and taken apart.

Depth of field grew a lens' worth of settings in the same release: focus that
follows a node, separate near and far ranges, aperture blades, and bokeh that
survives the blur.

---

## Screen shaders

Open the **Post Processing** node and there is a **screen shaders** list.
**+ Add screen shader**, pick a `.flsl`, and it runs over every frame.

Each pass has a switch, ▲▼ to reorder, and its own knobs — the ones the shader
declares, laid out the same way a material's are. Turning one off keeps it and
its settings; it just stops running.

A screen shader is a `.flsl` with `stage post`. It gets four things about the
frame, and every one of them can be read **at any pixel, not just the one being
drawn** — which is what makes an outline, a blur or a warp possible at all:

- **`sceneColor(uv)`** — the picture so far, in real light. This is upstream of
  the tonemap, so a bright light really does read brighter than white.
- **`sceneDepth(uv)`** — how far the camera is from what it sees, in world
  units. Sky reads a very large number, so an object against the sky is the
  biggest step in the frame.
- **`sceneNormal(uv)`** — which way the surface faces. Worked out from depth, so
  it exists for meshes, terrain, sculpted matter, tilemaps and animated
  characters alike, with nothing to import and nothing to set up.
- **`screenTexel()`** — the size of one pixel. Step by it to reach a neighbour.
  It follows the retro resolution, so a one-pixel line stays one pixel instead
  of being blown up by the upscale.

Leave the `uv` off and you get this pixel's — so a colour grade is one line.

Everything else the language already had comes with you: noise, `palette`,
`posterize`, `hueShift`, `contrast`, the full maths set. And each pass shows a
live thumbnail in the ◈ Shaders graph, over a little test scene of a ball in
front of a wall, so you can watch an outline appear as you build it.

**Where they run:** over the finished picture, after depth of field, and before
the colour grade, the lens and the film grain. So whatever you draw is treated
as part of the art — an outline gets graded and vignetted like everything else,
instead of being stencilled onto the top of the frame.

## Ink Outline

`shaders/examples/inkOutline.flsl` — the comic-book look. Add it and every object
in the scene gets a drawn edge. Nothing in the scene has to change.

- **thickness** — how far out the line looks, in pixels.
- **silhouette** — how eagerly it draws where something in front stops and
  something much further away begins.
- **crease** — how eagerly it draws where one flat face folds into another at the
  same distance. Depth alone can barely see those; the surface direction can.
- **softness** — 0 is a hard line, 1 fades it in like a brush.
- **ink colour** — and its *alpha* is how black the line goes. Pull it down for a
  sketch instead of a print.

It is commented line by line, including the part that matters most: it looks for
the **bend** in depth rather than the difference. A floor seen at a grazing angle
changes distance enormously from one pixel to the next while being perfectly
flat, and an outline that measures the plain difference paints it solid black.
Measuring the bend is zero across any flat surface at any angle, so the line
lands on real edges.

`shaders/examples/crtScanlines.flsl` is the short one — a dozen lines, a curved
tube, scan lines and a bezel. Read that one first if you want to write your own.

Both are seeded into new projects. Existing projects get them the next time you
open one (delete the examples folder and it stays deleted).

## From a script

```lua
local post = find("Post Processing")

-- switch a pass on and off; it keeps its settings either way
post:setScreenShader("inkOutline", bossFight)

-- and drive its knobs live, like any other shader uniform
post:setShaderParam("inkOutline.thickness", 1 + rage * 2)
post:setShaderParam("inkOutline.silhouette", 20)
```

The name is the file without its extension — the one the Inspector shows. Leave
the `inkOutline.` prefix off a parameter and it sets that knob on every pass;
pass `""` to `setScreenShader` and the whole authored look goes on or off at
once.

## Depth of field grew up

It was three settings — a distance, a range and a blur amount. It is now a lens.

- **Follow a node.** Pick one under the focus distance and the focus becomes the
  camera's distance to it, every frame. That's a rack focus: keep the character
  sharp while the world drifts, or point it at something else and watch the shot
  change. It's measured per view, so the Scene view stays focused on what *you*
  are looking at while you fly around.
- **Near and far are separate ranges.** A lens goes soft in front of the focus
  much sooner than behind it, and they're the two numbers you actually reach
  for: a portrait wants the foreground gone and the background readable. Leave
  the near range linked and it stays at half the far one, which is what the
  effect always did.
- **Aperture blades.** 0 is a round iris. 6 is the hexagonal bokeh of a real
  lens, and there's an angle to turn it with.
- **Highlight bokeh.** Bright points now spread into visible discs instead of
  averaging into grey. It works on light that is *brighter than white*, so it
  needs something genuinely bright — a specular glint, a lamp, a sun off water.
- **Samples.** More is smoother bokeh at a linear cost; fewer is the chunky look
  on purpose.
- **Show the focus band.** A tuning view: cool where the near side is going soft,
  warm where the far side is, the picture itself where it's sharp. Which side of
  the focus a pixel is on is the one thing you can't read off a blurred frame,
  and it is the thing you need in order to move the focus.

All of it is available from a script too — `dofFocus`, `dofRange`,
`dofNearRange`, `dofBlur`, `dofBlades`, `dofBladeAngle`, `dofHighlight`,
`dofSamples`:

```lua
-- a one-second rack focus onto whatever the player is aiming at
local post = find("Post Processing"):getcomponent("PostProcess")
post.dofFocus = lerp(post.dofFocus, aimDistance, dt * 4)
```

**Nothing to do on upgrade** — every new setting starts at the value that keeps
the old behaviour, including the near range.

## Every shader slot opens

Anywhere you can *pick* a shader you can now open it: a **◈** button next to the
Material's shader, the Skybox's, and each screen shader in the list. It goes
straight to the ◈ Shaders graph. Before this, changing a line of a shader meant
finding it again in the Assets panel every time.

## Also

- **The shader palette lists everything again.** The ◈ graph tab's node menu and
  the Scripting tab's autocomplete each kept their own list of categories, so an
  op in a new one worked perfectly and was simply never offered. They now read
  one list.
- A shader that reads `time` gets a live preview thumbnail in the graph editor.
  UI shaders were being told the clock didn't exist.

## Nothing to do on upgrade

A scene with no screen shaders renders exactly the frames it rendered before,
and saves the file it saved before — the list only appears in a scene once you
put something in it.

## Not in this one

A screen shader can't take its own texture yet: it gets the frame, its depth and
its normals, and nothing else. A colour ramp you supply is the obvious next step.
